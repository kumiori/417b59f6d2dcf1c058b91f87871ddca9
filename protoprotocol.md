Take the following protoprotocol for a game and propose a first embodiment draft for this current project 

MONX — Re-embodiment Protocol

> Transfer document for another agent: mechanics, logic, flexibility,
> genuine constraints.

0. Purpose

This document transfers the generative logic of the game, not a
fixed implementation. A new agent should be able to re-embody it in
another city, venue, community, theme, or final objective without merely
copying the Albatros example.

1. Starting condition

Begin with something desirable that does not yet exist: an event,
gathering, exhibition, publication, shared resource, journey, or
intervention.

There is a real container and real constraints, but the path toward it
is deliberately unfinished.

Canonical rule:

> There is no event yet. Every completed action can make it more
> possible. When enough real conditions become true, the game ends and
> the thing begins.

2. Core hypothesis

People and cities contain dispersed transformative capacity: time,
money, knowledge, attention, contacts, spaces, equipment, hospitality,
artistic propositions, curiosity, and care.

Much of it remains trapped in individuals because there is no light
mechanism for discovering, connecting, and directing it toward a shared
possibility.

The game is a membrane for making that capacity actionable.

3. The primitive loop

The reproducible engine is:

encounter → signal → mission → action → resource → threshold → newly
possible state → new mission

An encounter may begin from a QR code in the street, a conversation, an
event, a message, an object, or another player.

The player leaves a minimal signal. The system offers or reveals an
actionable mission. Completing the mission changes the shared state.
That change can unlock a new branch.

4. What counts as energy

Do not collapse energy into money. Money is one resource among several.

The game should be able to register heterogeneous forms of capacity
without pretending they are perfectly commensurable:

• money and material resources
• time and labour
• spaces, beds, food, transport, equipment
• skills and knowledge
• introductions and social connections
• artistic propositions and ideas
• attention, audience, presence
• needs and requests for support

A decisive move may therefore be €5, a sofa, an introduction, one hour
of work, a translation, a projector, a question, or the discovery of
somebody who needs precisely what another participant can offer.

5. Missions

Missions are small transformations of the current state.

They should be concrete enough to complete, meaningful enough to alter
the game, and diverse enough that participation is not reserved for
people with money, status, or specialist skills.

Examples:

• find a person with a needed skill
• offer a bed
• locate a poster site
• invite an artist
• obtain a piece of equipment
• translate a text
• bring one new player
• solve a logistical need
• contribute money
• formulate a missing question
• organize a micro-encounter
• carry an object from one point in the city to another

Important: missions should increasingly arise from the actual
consequences of previous actions, rather than from a complete mission
tree written in advance.

6. Thresholds

Thresholds connect play to reality.

A threshold is not merely a score. It corresponds to a condition after
which something becomes genuinely possible: enough budget for travel,
enough beds for guests, enough equipment for a performance, enough
participants for an intervention, enough confirmed content to open
another day.

Prefer several visible dimensions over a single percentage.

For example:

SPACE       ✓
BEDS        4 / 7
SOUND       72%
CASH        €1,460 / €3,200
PUBLIC      86 / 150
FOOD        ?

Progress should expose the anatomy of the emerging object rather than
pretending everything is one number.

7. Agency and genuine openness

The initiators may know the container, costs, legal limits, safety
constraints, and some initial possibilities.

They should not pretend that everything is open if it is not.

Conversely, the collective process must contain consequential degrees of
freedom: something participants do can actually change what happens.

Test for genuineness:

> What is the smallest consequential thing the initiating group is
> genuinely willing not to decide in advance?

If the answer is nothing, the system is recruitment or crowdfunding
dressed as participation.

If actions can alter content, connections, allocation, sequence, scale,
or form, collective agency is real.

8. Requests are first-class signals

The game is not only a machine for extracting contributions.

Participants must be able to express needs:

> I need a place to sleep.  
> I need €120.  
> I need three bodies.  
> I need lighting knowledge.  
> I need someone who speaks Arabic.

A powerful function of the system is matching available capacity with
unmet necessity.

9. City and physical world

The digital layer should remain porous to physical life.

QR codes can seed the game across the city, but they are only doors.

The game may unlock addresses, encounters, objects, performances,
conversations, or synchronized actions. A random street encounter should
be capable, in principle, of entering the same causal chain as a formal
contribution.

10. Collective heartbeat

Most play can be asynchronous.

Occasionally the distributed network can be made perceptible to itself
through a common moment: a short synchronized window to signal presence,
choose a branch, pledge a resource, respond to a question, or perform a
shared gesture.

This creates rhythm without requiring permanent central coordination.

11. Backstage governance

No central coordination is an aspiration for the visible collective
process, not a denial of operational responsibility.

Backstage actors may verify payments, feasibility, safety, contracts,
venue constraints, and commitments.

The interface should distinguish clearly between:

• hard constraints
• collective degrees of freedom

12. Irreversibility and fluidity

Actions leave traces. Once somebody contributes, introduces, builds,
promises, or creates, the path has changed and others may act upon it.

This is the useful sense in which the process is irreversible.

People themselves must remain fluid: they can reconsider positions,
withdraw future commitments where possible, change interests, and move
between roles.

13. Ending condition

The game should have an ontological ending, not merely a deadline.

When the minimum real conditions are satisfied, the hypothetical object
becomes executable.

The game announces that transition and then gets out of the way:

> **THE GAME IS OVER.**  
> **THE EVENT STARTS.**

The final gathering is both reward and evidence: dispersed capacities
have been transformed into a shared reality.

Any surplus capacity, money, or network should be handled according to
rules declared in advance and may, if participants choose, seed another
cycle.

14. What must remain flexible

The following are implementation choices, not invariants:

• the final object: festival, intervention, publication, journey,
common resource, etc.
• the geography: one venue, a neighborhood, a city, several cities
• the entry point: QR, conversation, performance, invitation, physical
token
• vocabulary and visual identity
• mission catalogue and how missions are generated
• resource dimensions and thresholds
• degree of anonymity or persistent identity
• whether branching decisions are individual, aggregated,
synchronized, or deliberative
• duration: hours, days, weeks, or months

15. What should survive every re-embodiment

1. A real desired state does not yet exist.
2. Small actions can alter its feasibility.
3. Multiple forms of energy are recognized.
4. Needs matter as much as offers.
5. Progress corresponds to real-world conditions.
6. Thresholds unlock genuinely new possibilities.
7. At least some outcomes are not predetermined.
8. The system reveals coordination rather than merely commanding it.
9. Digital signals reconnect to physical action.
10. The process has a legible ending in which play becomes reality.

16. Instruction to the receiving agent

Do not begin by copying screens, labels, missions, or the Albatros
festival.

First identify:

1. the desired-but-not-yet-real object;
2. its hard constraints;
3. its genuinely open variables;
4. the energies already dispersed around it;
5. the unmet needs;
6. the minimum conditions under which it becomes real.

Then design the smallest loop in which one encounter can change that
state.

Add complexity only when the emerging state requires it.

Treat every proposed mechanic as provisional. Ask:

> Does this mechanic reveal or connect agency, or does it merely gamify
> compliance?

> Does a participant’s action have a consequence?

> Can an unexpected person or resource change the path?

> Can the system discover possibilities the initiators did not encode?

Preserve these questions even when the implementation changes
completely.
